High-Performance (Multi-)Linear Algebra

From GEMM to tensor contractions — dense & sparse
Instructor: Jiajia Li
Fall 2026
11/11/2026
This is an advanced topic that ties together threads from the whole course: the GEMM you tuned in the midterm, the SpMV in the homeworks, GPU shared-memory tiling, and distributed SUMMA. The unifying idea is that matrix multiply is just the 2-D case of a much larger family — multi-linear algebra on tensors. The dense half surveys how the community reaches near-peak on dense tensor contractions; the sparse half is my own research area, so we will spend real time on formats, kernels, and the systems built around them.

Module 1
From GEMM to Multi-Linear Algebra

The Ladder: GEMM → Batched → Contraction

GEMMThe BLAS-3 workhorse: \( C_{ij} = \sum_k A_{ik} B_{kj} \). The single most tuned kernel in computing — you climbed this ladder in the midterm.
Batched GEMMMany small, independent products at once: \( C^{(b)} = A^{(b)} B^{(b)} \). Fills the machine when one GEMM is too small (deep learning, block solvers).
Tensor contractionSum over shared indices of higher-order arrays, e.g. \( C_{abc} = \sum_{k} A_{akc}\,B_{kb} \). GEMM is just the order-2 special case.
Same algebra, rising dimensionality.
The key mental model for the whole topic: a tensor contraction is a sum over one or more shared ("contracted") index groups, leaving the "free" indices of each operand on the result. Set the order to 2 and you recover GEMM. Everything the community does for tensors is an attempt to reuse the decades of GEMM engineering at higher order.

Notation — Einstein Summation

\(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B},\mathcal{X}\)  order-N tensor order = #indices (modes) free index  appears in output contracted index  summed away
Einsum, informallyName every index; repeated names on the input side that are absent from the output are summed.
\( C_{abc} = \sum_{k,l} A_{akl}\,B_{lbkc} \)

Contraction generalizes matrix multiply, matrix–vector product, inner/outer products, and mode-n products all at once.

Two flagship kernels (sparse world) TTM / SpTTM — tensor × matrix along one mode:
\( \mathcal{Y}_{ijr} = \sum_{k}\mathcal{X}_{ijk}\,U_{kr} \)
MTTKRP — matricized tensor × Khatri–Rao product (the CP hot loop):
\( \tilde{A} = X_{(1)}\,(C \odot B) \)
Students should leave this slide able to read an einsum expression and identify which indices survive to the output (free) and which are summed (contracted). TTM and MTTKRP are the two kernels we will return to on the sparse side — TTM is the engine of Tucker, MTTKRP is the engine of CP.

How Do You Make a Contraction Fast? TTGT vs. GETT

TTGT — Transpose-Transpose-GEMM-TransposePermute & flatten each tensor into a matrix, call tuned GEMM, then transpose the result back. Simple & portable — reuses all of BLAS.
Cost of TTGTThe explicit transposes move data with no flops and need scratch memory — pure overhead, worst for "fat" contractions with little arithmetic.
GETT — GEMM-like Tensor-Tensor mult.Fold the reshaping into the packing/blocking of a GEMM-style macro-kernel. No explicit transpose, no scratch — the permutation happens for free while data is copied into tiles anyway.
Also: LoG"Loops-over-GEMM" — wrap batched GEMM calls in loops over the remaining indices. A useful middle ground.
The recurring themeThe cache/tiling lessons from Topic 7 come back exactly: whoever packs data into cache-sized tiles with the fewest wasted moves wins. TTGT is the naïve copy; GETT is the fused, tiled version.
Springer & Bientinesi, "Design of a High-Performance GEMM-like Tensor-Tensor Multiplication," ACM TOMS 44(3), 2018.
Draw the analogy to tiled matmul from the cache lecture: TTGT is like materializing a transposed copy of B before multiplying, GETT is like transposing-on-the-fly inside the packing routine. TBLIS and GETT are the two clean "no-transpose" designs; cuTENSOR uses similar ideas on the GPU.
Module 2
Dense Tensor Contraction Frameworks

TBLIS — Contraction Without Transposition

Built on BLIS (the GotoBLAS-style GEMM framework you saw behind tuned matmul). TBLIS realizes the GETT idea in full.

Key ideaFuse the logical tensor transposition into BLIS's own partitioning & packing stages via a "block-scatter-matrix" view of the tensor. No permutation pass, no workspace.
ResultArbitrary contractions run at performance approaching the equivalent GEMM — the transpose overhead simply disappears.
block-scatter-matrix idea

// A tensor's modes are split into
//   M-group (free, rows)
//   N-group (free, cols)
//   K-group (contracted)
// A "scatter" vector maps each logical
// (i,j) to the real memory offset, so
// BLIS packs tiles directly from the
// tensor — transpose happens for free.

One caveat: near-square contractions benefit most; very "thin" cases still need care.

D. A. Matthews, "High-Performance Tensor Contraction without Transposition," SIAM J. Sci. Comput. 40(1):C1–C24, 2018.
TBLIS is the cleanest existence proof that you never need to physically transpose. The scatter/block-scatter vectors are the trick: they let the BLIS packing kernels read from an arbitrarily-strided tensor as if it were a matrix. Devin Matthews comes from quantum chemistry, where these contractions dominate coupled-cluster methods.

Cyclops Tensor Framework (CTF) — Distributed

Scales tensor contractions to distributed memory (thousands of nodes) — the MPI/SUMMA world of Topic 11, generalized to order-N.

Cyclic distributionSpreads tensor elements cyclically across the process grid → even load and packed storage even for symmetric tensors.
Why symmetry mattersQuantum-chemistry tensors have permutational symmetry; cyclic layout keeps the compressed form balanced across ranks.
Contractions as communicationCTF reduces each contraction to a distributed matrix multiply and picks a mapping that minimizes communication — the same cost model as SUMMA/Cannon, one dimension up.
Flagship useCoupled-cluster electronic-structure codes (e.g. CCSD/CCSDT) — some of the largest tensor contractions run in science.
Solomonik, Matthews, Hammond, Demmel, "Cyclops Tensor Framework…," IPDPS 2013; journal version in J. Parallel Distrib. Comput. 74(12), 2014.
Tie CTF back to the distributed-memory lectures: it is literally SUMMA-style 2.5D/redistribution logic applied to contractions, plus symmetry-aware packed storage. Edgar Solomonik's communication-avoiding background shows up in the mapping choices. Good place to remind students that "tensor contraction = matmul after the right reshape/redistribute."

cuTENSOR — Tensor Primitives on the GPU

NVIDIA's GPU library exposing an Einstein-summation API. The "cuBLAS of tensors," using Tensor Cores & mixed precision.

Three operation classes • Contraction (generalized GEMM)
• Reduction (sum/max over modes)
• Element-wise with operator fusion (permute + scale + add in one pass)
Where it's usedBackend for cuTensorNet in the cuQuantum SDK (quantum-circuit simulation) and for einsum in deep-learning frameworks.
Same tiling story on-GPUPicks contraction algorithms & tile shapes to fill Tensor Cores — the GPU shared-memory tiling of Topic 8, automated.
NVIDIA cuTENSOR docs (developer.nvidia.com/cutensor); Bayraktar et al., "cuQuantum SDK," arXiv:2308.01999, 2023 (cuTensorNet uses cuTENSOR as its contraction backend).
cuTENSOR is the vendor answer: you give it the index strings and strides, it selects a plan (which may internally permute) and runs on Tensor Cores. The important connection for a quantum audience is cuTensorNet/cuQuantum, where contracting a tensor network IS the simulation. Note it also does transposition/permutation as first-class ops.

Bientinesi's Line — GETT & the Linnea Compiler

GETT (kernel level)Identify the three index groups of a contraction and drive a tuned GEMM-style macro-kernel — no explicit transpose. Shipped with TTGT & LoG in the open TCCG code generator, so you can compare all three.
Springer & Bientinesi, ACM TOMS 44(3), 2018.
Linnea (expression level)A compiler for linear-algebra expressions: given a high-level formula, it exploits matrix properties & algebraic identities to emit an efficient sequence of BLAS/LAPACK calls — beating a naïve one-call-per-operation translation.
Barthels, Psarras, Bientinesi, "Linnea…," ACM TOMS 47(3), 2021.
Why include this groupThey span the two altitudes that matter: the micro-kernel (GETT, like your GEMM tuning) and the whole-expression optimizer (Linnea, like a query planner for matrix math). Their survey "The Landscape of Software for Tensor Computations" (Psarras, Karlsson, Bientinesi, 2021) is a good map of the whole field.
Bientinesi's HPAC group is worth showing because it connects the two ends of the stack. GETT is the low-level answer to "how do I contract fast"; Linnea is the high-level answer to "given a big matrix expression, what order and which kernels?" — essentially a cost-based compiler. The 2021 survey is a good pointer for the curious.

Dense Landscape — One Table

FrameworkScopeTrick
TBLIS1 node, CPUBLIS packing, no transpose

cuTENSORGPUTensor Cores, fused einsum
GETT / TCCG1 node, CPUGEMM-like macro-kernel

The common threadEvery one of them is "reach GEMM performance at order N" — by avoiding transposes, balancing across nodes, or filling Tensor Cores. The dense world is, at heart, solved by reduction to GEMM.
…and that's exactly what breaks nextReduction-to-GEMM assumes dense, regular data. Real tensors — reviews, health records, graphs — are hyper-sparse. There is no dense matrix to call GEMM on.
Use this as the pivot slide. The dense story has a happy ending: everything reduces to GEMM. The sparse story does not — the moment >99.99% of entries are zero, materializing a dense matricization is impossible, and all the format and locality problems come roaring back. That is the research half of the lecture.
Module 3
Sparse Tensors — Formats & Kernels

Why Sparse Tensors?

A tensor is a multi-way array; real data is naturally multi-way:

Examples• (user × item × time) reviews
• (patient × diagnosis × procedure) health records
• (source × dest × port) network traffic
Extreme sparsityOften < 0.01% nonzero. A dense representation is astronomically too big — you must store only nonzeros, like SpMV in the homeworks but one dimension up.
Why it's hard (vs. SpMV) • No dense GEMM to fall back on.
• Indexing overhead grows with order.
• Irregular access → poor locality & load imbalance.
• The best format depends on the tensor and the kernel and the machine.

This is the SpMV format-selection problem (CSR vs. ELL vs. …) made worse by dimensionality.

Motivating data & workloads: Li et al., PASTA benchmark suite, 2019.
Anchor to the homework SpMV: students already know that sparse matrices need special formats and that the best format is input-dependent. Sparse tensors are the same story with more indices, more overhead, and a format-choice space that explodes. Motivate with the FROSTT-style real datasets (reviews, health, network).

Format 1 — COO (Coordinate)

\(nnz\)  number of nonzeros mode = tensor dimension "mode-agnostic"  no mode preferred

Store each nonzero as (i, j, k, value). The tensor analog of the simplest sparse-matrix format.

ProsDead simple, mode-agnostic (no index is privileged), easy to build.
ConsStores an integer index per mode per nonzero → for order-4 that's 4 indices for 1 value. Poor locality; lots of redundant index bytes.
COO for an order-3 tensor

i:   [0 0 1 2 2]
j:   [0 2 1 0 3]
k:   [1 0 2 1 0]
val: [a b c d e]
// 3 index arrays + 1 value array,
// all of length nnz

Index storage dominates: 3–4× the value bytes for high-order tensors.

COO is the baseline everyone compares against. Its virtue — mode-agnosticism — matters because tensor kernels operate along different modes, and mode-specific formats (like CSF) pay a cost to switch modes. Its vice is index bloat, which HiCOO attacks directly.

Format 2 — CSF (Compressed Sparse Fiber)

Generalizes CSR to tensors: nonzeros form a tree, one level per mode, sharing common index prefixes.

IdeaA "fiber" is a mode-1 slice (fix all but one index). CSF compresses repeated outer indices — like CSR row pointers, nested N deep.
Foundational workThe CSF format & the parallel SPLATT library are the reference point the whole field builds on.
CSF tree: one level per mode.
Smith & Karypis, CSF format (IA³ 2015); Smith, Ravindran, Sidiropoulos, Karypis, "SPLATT: Efficient and Parallel Sparse Tensor-Matrix Multiplication," IPDPS 2015.
Attribute CSF and SPLATT clearly to Smith and Karypis — this is the foundational format the community, including my own group, builds on and compares against. The tree view makes the CSR analogy concrete: outer indices are shared like CSR row pointers, but nested to tensor order. Downside: it's mode-specific — you may store several trees to contract along different modes.

Format 3 — HiCOO (Hierarchical COO)

block = small dense-ish sub-tensor byte-index = offset within a block goal: keep COO's mode-agnostic simplicity, shrink the bytes

Compress COO indices in units of sparse blocks: store each block's origin once, then a tiny byte-sized offset per nonzero within the block.

Best of bothKeeps COO's mode-agnostic simplicity and slashes index bytes; blocking also improves data locality.
Payoff (MTTKRP)Up to 23× over COO and up to 15.6× over CSF, with less or comparable storage.
Blocked indices → fewer bytes, more locality.
Li, Sun, Vuduc, "HiCOO: Hierarchical Storage of Sparse Tensors," SC 2018.
HiCOO (pronounced "haiku") is my SC18 work. The one-line pitch: COO wastes bytes on full-width indices; if you block the tensor, most of each index is the block origin (shared) and only a small within-block offset varies, which fits in a byte. You get COO's mode-agnostic flexibility, far smaller footprint, and better locality — hence the big MTTKRP speedups over both COO and CSF.

Format 4 — ALTO (Adaptive Linearized Tensor Order)

linearize = map (i,j,k,…) → one integer mode-agnostic  same layout serves every mode adaptive bit-encoding  bits per mode ∝ its extent

Encode each nonzero's whole coordinate as a single linearized integer that interleaves the mode bits, so spatially-near nonzeros stay near in memory — along every mode at once.

One copy, all modesRuns MTTKRP for every mode from a single tensor copy — no per-mode reordering, no per-mode duplicate structures.
WinsAn adaptive bit-encoding trades cheap index arithmetic for smaller metadata & better bandwidth use; cuts load imbalance and synchronization.
FormatModeStrategy
COOagnosticraw coordinates
CSFspecificper-mode tree
HiCOOagnosticblocking
ALTOagnosticlinearization

CSF is mode-specific (may store one tree per mode); HiCOO stays agnostic via blocking; ALTO stays agnostic via linearization of the coordinate space.

Helal, Laukemann, Checconi, Tithi, Ranganathan, Petrini, Sadayappan, "ALTO: Adaptive Linearized Storage of Sparse Tensors," ICS 2021.
ALTO is the contrast piece to HiCOO. Both are mode-agnostic — they don't privilege a mode the way CSF's tree does — but they get there differently: HiCOO blocks the coordinate space and stores byte offsets within blocks; ALTO linearizes the full coordinate into one integer with an adaptive number of bits per mode, so a space-filling-curve- like ordering keeps neighbors close along all modes simultaneously. The payoff is one tensor copy that serves MTTKRP for every mode with low imbalance and synchronization — no per-mode copies or reordering. Note the author list includes Jee Choi (a frequent collaborator) and P. Sadayappan.

The Hot Kernels — MTTKRP & SpTTM

MTTKRPMatricized-tensor times Khatri–Rao product — the dominant cost of CP decomposition.
\( \tilde{A} = X_{(1)}\,(C \odot B) \)
Touches every nonzero once per mode; memory-bound and irregular.
SpTTM / TTMSparse tensor times dense matrix along a mode — the engine of Tucker.
\( \mathcal{Y}_{ijr} = \sum_k \mathcal{X}_{ijk}\,U_{kr} \)
Why they're hard in parallel• Output-side write conflicts (atomics / privatization)
• Irregular reuse of the dense factor rows
• Severe load imbalance across fibers/slices
• A different mode = a different access pattern
Same tricks returnTiling, privatization/reduction (false-sharing lesson!), reordering for locality, format specialization.
MTTKRP as CP bottleneck: Li et al., IPDPS 2017 & SC 2018. SpTTM: Li et al., IA³/HPEC 2016; Ma et al., J. Parallel Distrib. Comput. 129, 2019.
These two kernels are why formats matter — they are the innermost loops of the two dominant decompositions. MTTKRP for CP, TTM for Tucker. Every parallelization headache from earlier lectures reappears: write conflicts (use privatization/reduction, exactly the false-sharing fix), load imbalance, and locality. That is what makes them a rich systems research target.

Making Kernels Faster — Reordering & Layout

Reordering for localityRelabel indices so nearby nonzeros cluster — turning irregular access into blocked, cache-friendly access. Two schemes: BFS-MCS (maximum-cardinality-search heuristic) and Lexi-Order (doubly-lexical ordering lifted to tensors).
PayoffUp to 4.1× sequential and 11.9× parallel speedup on HiCOO-MTTKRP — for free, as preprocessing.
GPU data layout for SpTTMFine thread granularity, coalesced access, rank blocking, shared memory, and an in-place scheme that avoids reorganizing the tensor ↔ matrix.
ResultGPU-SpTTM up to ~67× over a CPU baseline on P100.
Li et al., "Efficient and Effective Sparse Tensor Reordering," ICS 2019. Ma, Li et al., "Optimizing Sparse Tensor Times Matrix on GPUs," JPDC 129, 2019.
Reordering is the tensor version of the cache-locality transformations from Topic 7 — you cannot change the hardware, but relabeling indices changes the access pattern and recovers most of the lost locality, and it composes with HiCOO's blocking. The GPU SpTTM work is the coalescing/shared-memory/in-place story from the GPU lectures, applied to a sparse kernel.
Module 4
Why It All Matters — Tensor Decompositions

CP and Tucker — the "SVD for Tensors"

CP / CANDECOMP-PARAFACApproximate a tensor as a sum of R rank-1 terms:
\( \mathcal{X} \approx \sum_{r=1}^{R} a_r \circ b_r \circ c_r \)
Solved by CP-ALS — whose per-iteration cost is a sequence of MTTKRPs.
TuckerA small dense core tensor times a factor matrix per mode:
\( \mathcal{X} \approx \mathcal{G}\times_1 A\times_2 B\times_3 C \)
Solved via HOOI — inner loop is TTM.
The whole pointDecompositions do data mining, compression, and latent-factor discovery. Their runtime is dominated by MTTKRP (CP) and TTM (Tucker) — so all the format/kernel work pays off directly here.
Redundancy across MTTKRPsAdaTM memoizes shared partial results across the MTTKRP sequence in a CP-ALS sweep — a model-driven space/time tradeoff, up to 8× over SPLATT.
AdaTM: Li, Choi, Perros, Sun, Vuduc, "Model-Driven Sparse CP Decomposition for Higher-Order Tensors," IPDPS 2017.
Close the loop: the reason anyone cares about MTTKRP and TTM is that they are the inner loops of CP and Tucker, the workhorse tensor decompositions for analytics. AdaTM is a nice algorithmic twist — within one CP-ALS iteration you compute an MTTKRP per mode, and they share sub-computations; memoizing them (with a model to decide how much to cache) cuts asymptotic work. It is built as a patch on SPLATT, reusing CSF.

Input-Adaptive & Auto-Tuned Approaches

InTensLi — dense TTMGenerates fast, in-place dense tensor-times-matrix for arbitrary order/mode, avoiding the explicit tensor→matrix conversion — an input-adaptive framework that tunes the loop & parallel parameters.
Li, Battaglino, Perros, Sun, Vuduc, SC 2015.
SMAT — the SpMV ancestorAuto-tunes the sparse-matrix format (CSR/ELL/…) per input via an offline-trained model — the format-selection idea that tensors inherit.
Li, Tan, Chen, Sun, "SMAT," PLDI 2013.
The unifying ideaThere is no single best format or schedule. The winning system measures the input (and machine) and adapts — format, blocking, thread granularity, memoization.
Connects back to the midtermSame philosophy as auto-tuned GEMM/BLAS: a tuned search over implementations beats one hand-fixed kernel.
Two threads meet here. InTensLi is the dense-TTM, in-place, input-adaptive engine (SC15). SMAT (PLDI13) is the older SpMV auto-tuner that established "learn the best sparse format for this input" — the intellectual ancestor of format selection for tensors. The common message: adaptivity beats a single fixed kernel, exactly as with tuned BLAS.

Systems & Benchmarks — the ParTI! Ecosystem

ParTI! / HiParTIA parallel sparse-tensor infrastructure (CPU+GPU): COO/HiCOO storage, MTTKRP, SpTTM, and CP/Tucker building blocks. The library home of much of this work.
PASTAThe first benchmark suite for sparse-tensor kernels — representative workloads so you can compare systems & architectures fairly.
SpartaHigh-performance element-wise sparse tensor contraction on heterogeneous (DRAM + NVM) memory — extends the story to general contraction, not just TTM/MTTKRP.
Why a benchmark mattersSparse-tensor performance is input-dependent; without a common suite (PASTA), speedup claims aren't comparable. Reproducible workloads are how the field makes real progress — a systems-engineering lesson beyond tensors.
PASTA: Li, Ma, Wu, Li, Barker, CCF Trans. HPC / arXiv:1902.03317, 2019. Sparta: Liu, Ren, Gioiosa, Li, Li, PPoPP 2021. ParTI!/HiParTI: github.com/hpcgarage/ParTI.
Wrap the systems tour: ParTI!/HiParTI is the library where HiCOO, the kernels, and reordering live; PASTA is the benchmark suite that makes cross-system comparison meaningful; Sparta pushes into general element-wise contraction on heterogeneous memory. The meta-point — you need a shared benchmark before performance claims mean anything — is a good systems lesson in its own right.

Takeaways

Big Picture — What to Remember

It's all GEMM, generalizedContractions are the multi-linear GEMM; batched GEMM is the bridge.
Dense = reduce to GEMMTBLIS/GETT avoid transposes; CTF distributes; cuTENSOR fills Tensor Cores.
Sparse breaks thatNo dense matrix to call — format & locality become the whole game.
Format is a first-class choiceCOO → CSF → HiCOO; the best one is input-, kernel-, and machine-dependent.
Kernels drive appsMTTKRP → CP, TTM → Tucker. Speed the kernel, speed the science.
Old lessons returnTiling, coalescing, privatization, NUMA, auto-tuning — all reappear one order up.
One sentence Multi-linear algebra is where every performance idea in this course — locality, tiling, GPU coalescing, distributed layout, avoiding false sharing, and auto-tuning — comes back at once, because a tensor is just a matrix with more places to lose reuse.
The synthesis slide. If students remember one thing: contractions generalize GEMM; the dense world wins by reducing to GEMM; the sparse world can't, so formats and locality dominate; and every optimization technique from the course reappears. This is deliberately a capstone that reaches back to caches, GPUs, and distributed memory.
